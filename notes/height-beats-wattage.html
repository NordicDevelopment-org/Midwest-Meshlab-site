Why height beats wattage every time

Jun 12, 2026Read 4 minTags RF · Range

We spent a Saturday trying to brute-force range with power, and a different Saturday raising an antenna a few meters. The mast won, and it wasn't close. Here's why that keeps happening, and what we actually measured.

LoRa is patient; terrain is not

LoRa is already extraordinarily good at digging a weak signal out of the noise — that's the whole point of the modulation. So once you're link-budget-limited, pouring on more transmit power buys you a little, but the radio was rarely the bottleneck. Obstructions were. A hill, a tree line, or the curve of the earth doesn't care how loud you are.

Line of sight does most of the work

Get both antennas up where they can "see" each other and range opens up dramatically. Raising a node from bench height to the top of a short mast clears the near-field clutter — fences, cars, your own roofline — that quietly eats the first hop. In flat country especially, every meter of height pushes the horizon out and adds nodes you simply could not hear before.

The rule of thumb

Spend your first dollar on height, your second on a better antenna and a clean feedline, and only then think about power. Most "dead" links are really just blocked ones.

What we measured

Same two nodes, same firmware, same day. On the bench-to-bench test we struggled to hold a link across town. Put one node on a five-meter painter's pole with a clear shot down the valley and the same pair held a solid link many kilometers out — a multiple of the bench result, with margin to spare. We didn't touch the transmit power once.

Practical takeaways

  • Mount relays as high as you safely can — rooftops, masts, and tall structures earn their keep.
  • Keep the antenna vertical and the feedline short; a bad connector undoes a good location.
  • Plan hops around what each node can see, not how far apart they are on a map.
  • When a link won't hold, raise it before you turn it up.
